December is when many business owners write their biggest checks to church and charity. The tax result depends on a handful of decisions made before December 31, and the rules changed for 2026.

Scripture puts giving first, not last: "Honor the LORD with your wealth and with the firstfruits of all your produce" (Proverbs 3:9, ESV). Planning the gift well is part of honoring it.

This article is general information about common year-end giving decisions. It is not tax advice for a particular business or household.

## What changed for 2026

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in July 2025, changed how charitable gifts are deducted starting with 2026 returns.

**A deduction for people who do not itemize.** If you take the standard deduction, you can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to qualifying charities ($2,000 on a joint return). Gifts to donor-advised funds generally do not count toward this deduction, and gifts of property do not either.

**A floor for people who itemize.** Itemized charitable gifts are deductible only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of adjusted gross income. With $200,000 of AGI, the first $1,000 of gifts produces no itemized deduction.

**A cap for the top bracket.** For taxpayers in the 37% bracket, the tax benefit of itemized deductions, including charitable gifts, is limited to about 35 cents per dollar.

**A floor for C corporations.** A C corporation's charitable deduction is now allowed only above 1% of taxable income, and still no more than 10%.

## Standard deduction or itemize: decide before you give

For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, and $24,150 for heads of household. If your mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and gifts do not clear that amount, your giving does not lower your tax beyond the new $1,000 or $2,000 cash deduction.

That is where **bunching** helps. Instead of giving the same amount every year, you combine two or three years of planned giving into one year, itemize that year, and take the standard deduction in the off years.

A **donor-advised fund** makes bunching practical. You contribute to the fund this year and take the deduction this year, subject to the usual limits. Then you recommend grants to your church and other charities over the next several years on your normal schedule. One caution: in an off year when you take the standard deduction, contributions to the fund itself do not count toward the new non-itemizer deduction.

## Give appreciated stock instead of cash

If you have stock, mutual fund shares, or other assets held more than one year that have grown in value, giving the asset can beat giving cash. When you itemize, you can generally deduct the fair market value, and neither you nor the charity pays capital gains tax on the growth.

The limits differ. Gifts of appreciated long-term property to public charities are generally capped at 30% of AGI, compared with 60% for cash, with a five-year carryforward for the excess. Do not give assets that have lost value. Sell them, claim the loss, and give the cash.

Give the asset itself. Have the broker transfer the shares directly to the charity or donor-advised fund. If you sell first and donate the proceeds, you owe the gain tax.

## Give through the business, or personally?

The answer depends on how the business is taxed.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs.** A charitable gift is not a Schedule C business expense. It is deducted personally.

**S corporations and partnerships.** The business can write the check, but the gift passes through to the owners on their Schedule K-1 and is deducted on the owners' personal returns, under the same personal limits.

**C corporations.** The corporation deducts the gift on its own return, now only above the 1% floor and up to 10% of taxable income. Amounts lost to the floor generally carry forward only in a year when gifts also exceed the 10% ceiling. A corporation that gives a little every year may be better served by giving in larger, less frequent amounts.

**Sponsorship is not the same as a donation.** Sponsoring a ballfield banner or buying an ad in a charity program can be an ordinary business expense, often advertising, rather than a charitable contribution, when there is a real business purpose. A gift with nothing expected in return is a donation. Keep the agreement or invoice that shows which one it was.

## Qualified charitable distributions for owners 70½ and older

If you are 70½ or older and have a traditional IRA, a **qualified charitable distribution** lets you send money directly from the IRA to a charity. The amount is left out of your income entirely, and it can count toward your required minimum distribution. For 2026, the limit is $111,000 per person.

A QCD helps whether or not you itemize, and it lowers AGI. The money must go straight from the IRA custodian to the charity. Donor-advised funds and private foundations do not qualify. Ask the custodian early. December requests can take time to process.

## Keep the receipts the IRS expects

Good records protect a good gift.

- **Any cash gift:** keep a bank record, canceled check, or written receipt from the charity.
- **$250 or more in a single gift:** you need a written acknowledgment from the charity, received by the time you file, stating the amount and whether you received anything in return.
- **Noncash gifts over $500:** file Form 8283 with the return.
- **Noncash gifts over $5,000:** a qualified appraisal is generally required. Publicly traded stock is the common exception.

## When a gift counts for 2026

A gift counts in the year it is delivered.

- **Check:** counts on the date you mail it, so a check mailed by December 31 counts for 2026.
- **Credit card:** counts in the year the charge is made, even if you pay the card in January.
- **Online or pay-by-phone from a bank account:** counts on the date the bank pays it.
- **Stock:** counts when the shares reach the charity's account, not when you call the broker. Start transfers in early December.

A pledge is not a gift. Only what is actually paid by December 31 counts.

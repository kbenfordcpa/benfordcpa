The last weeks of the year are the best time to get your books in order. Clean records make tax preparation faster, help you spot problems while they can still be fixed, and give you a clear picture of how your business actually did. Here is a practical checklist business owners can work through before the year closes.

## 1. Reconcile every account

Start with the accounts that have a statement from someone else. Your bookkeeping should match them.

- **Bank accounts:** Reconcile each checking and savings account through the most recent statement. Look into old uncleared checks and deposits that never showed up.
- **Credit cards:** Reconcile each business card to its statement, including cards that only see occasional use.
- **Loans and lines of credit:** Compare the balance in your books to the lender's statement. If they don't match, the difference usually points to payments that were recorded incorrectly.

If an account has not been reconciled in months, start with the oldest unreconciled statement and work forward.

## 2. Gather receipts and mileage records

Good records support your deductions if questions ever come up.

- Keep receipts or other supporting records for your business expenses, and give extra attention to meals, travel, vehicle, and equipment costs.
- Save digital copies in one place, named so you can find them later.
- Review your mileage log for business trips. If your records are incomplete, don't try to recreate a log from memory. Gather what you do have (calendars, invoices, and other records made at the time) and talk with your CPA about how to handle the gap. Then set up a simple tracking method for next year.

## 3. Separate personal and business activity

Keeping personal and business money separate makes your records clearer and easier to support.

- Flag any personal expenses paid from business accounts, and any business expenses paid personally.
- Record owner activity correctly. Depending on how your business is set up, money you take out or put in may be a draw, a distribution, a contribution, or a loan. Draws, distributions, contributions, and loan principal are not operating expenses. Owner compensation paid through payroll is different: wages and related payroll costs need proper payroll treatment and may be deductible, subject to the applicable rules. Keep payroll and distributions clearly separate in your books.
- If you are unsure how a transaction should be classified, note it for your CPA instead of guessing.

## 4. Review receivables and payables

- **Accounts receivable:** Look at unpaid customer invoices. Follow up on past-due balances and identify any that are truly uncollectible.
- **Accounts payable:** Confirm which bills are still unpaid and that each is recorded once.

A caveat: how these items affect your taxes depends on your accounting method. Under the cash method, income is generally recognized when received and expenses are generally deducted when paid, with some exceptions. Under the accrual method, timing works differently. Writing off a bad debt also depends on whether the income was ever reported. Ask your CPA before making year-end moves based on timing.

## 5. Organize payroll and contractor records

- Confirm that payroll totals in your books match your payroll reports for the year.
- Make sure you have a current Form W-9 on file for each contractor you paid, and review the totals paid to each one.
- Check that employee and contractor names, addresses, and tax ID numbers are accurate before year-end forms are prepared.

Your payroll provider or CPA can confirm which forms are needed and when they are due.

## 6. Update fixed assets and loans

- List equipment, vehicles, furniture, and software purchased this year, with dates, costs, and invoices.
- Note anything that was sold, traded in, or disposed of.
- Split each loan payment between principal and interest. Interest may be deductible, but principal reduces the loan balance and is not an expense. Your lender's year-end statement can help.

## 7. Review unusual balances

Run a balance sheet and a profit and loss report and look for anything that seems off:

- Negative balances in bank or asset accounts
- Large amounts sitting in "uncategorized" or "ask my accountant"
- Big swings in expense categories compared to last year
- Old balances in suspense or clearing accounts

These items often reveal duplicate entries, missed transactions, or misclassified activity.

## 8. Prepare an organized handoff for your CPA

When your books are reconciled, put together a simple package:

- Year-end balance sheet and profit and loss
- Bank, credit card, and loan statements through December
- Payroll reports and contractor payment totals
- Asset purchase and sale details
- A short list of open questions and unusual transactions

An organized handoff saves time and leads to better conversations about planning for next year.

If your books feel messy, your software no longer fits how you operate, or you are still tracking everything in a spreadsheet, the start of the year is the easiest time to set up a proper accounting system or switch to a new one. For calendar-year business owners, January 1 gives you a clean break: last year's activity stays in last year's records, and 2027 starts fresh.

## Why January 1 is the easiest switch point

A mid-year switch is possible, but it usually means more work. You have to split one year's activity across two systems, then stitch reports together for tax preparation and other year-to-date reporting.

January 1 avoids most of that. Balance sheet accounts carry forward as opening balances, income and expense accounts start at zero, and bank feeds, payroll, and contractor tracking can begin cleanly for 2027. If your business uses a fiscal year other than the calendar year, the same idea applies on the first day of that year.

Those opening balances are only as good as the records they come from. If you are still working through year-end reconciliations, start with the [year-end bookkeeping checklist for business owners](/blog/year-end-bookkeeping-checklist-business-owners-florence). A clean close in 2026 is what makes a clean start in 2027 possible.

## What to bring over

A new system does not become reliable just because you connect a bank feed. Bring over the right information.

### Opening balances

Enter balance sheet accounts as of January 1, 2027, so they match the December 31, 2026 ending balances after accounts have been reconciled. The prior year does not have to be formally closed, but remaining adjustments should be small and documented.

Include:

- Bank and cash accounts
- Accounts receivable and accounts payable, if those accounts are part of your books
- Inventory, if you carry it
- Fixed assets and accumulated depreciation
- Credit cards, loans, and lines of credit
- Payroll liabilities still unpaid at year-end, such as tax deposits due in January
- Equity accounts, including prior-year retained earnings or owner's equity

Do not enter a full year of 2026 income and expense into the 2027 file just to have history there. That work belongs in the prior-year books.

### Open accounts receivable and accounts payable

If you invoice customers or enter bills, bring over the open items, not just a lump-sum total. For each unpaid invoice or bill, include the customer or vendor, date, number, original amount, and amount still outstanding. A lump-sum opening balance may make the balance sheet look right on January 1, but it makes payments hard to apply later and aging reports unreliable.

How these items appear on your financials depends on your accounting method. Under the cash method, they may be tracked for management even when they are not recognized for tax. If you are unsure what belongs on your January 1 balance sheet, ask your CPA before you enter the balances.

### Chart of accounts

Bring a chart of accounts that matches how the business actually operates, not every account you have ever created. Before you migrate, merge or remove unused and duplicate accounts, rename vague accounts such as "miscellaneous" or "ask my accountant" where you can identify the real activity, and map old accounts to new ones so monthly comparisons still make sense.

### Payroll and 1099 vendor information

January 1 is a natural reset because year-to-date totals start at zero. Bring over employee setup and pay types, unpaid payroll liabilities as of December 31, and a current Form W-9 and tax ID for each contractor you expect to pay. Check vendor names and addresses before prior-year 1099s are prepared.

Prior-year 1099s and W-2s generally come from the prior year's records. If you also change payroll providers, confirm who will file the 2026 forms and that the new provider starts with zero year-to-date figures. Keep the 2026 file available until those forms are done.

### Bank rules and recurring items

Review bank rules, recurring invoices and bills, memorized transactions, and connected apps before you turn them on in the new file. Fix rules that have been sending expenses to the wrong account instead of importing them. Plan the cutover so activity does not post to both systems, or to neither, in early January.

### Prior-year closed books

Keep the old system, or a complete backup, accessible for at least a full tax cycle, and longer if you may need historical detail later. Do not cancel the old subscription or delete the old file the week you switch. You usually do not need to re-enter years of old transactions; converting a lot of history can also bring old errors forward. For many business owners, solid opening balances plus access to the archived file are enough.

## Common setup mistakes

These problems show up later in reconciliations and tax prep:

- Going live before the prior-year books are close enough to trust
- Entering only the bank balances and skipping receivables, payables, loans, fixed assets, or equity
- Accepting bank-feed transactions already included in the opening balance, which creates duplicates
- Importing a messy chart of accounts as-is
- Entering the same January activity in both the old file and the new one
- Skipping the first-month reconciliation in the new system
- Changing your accounting method, entity type, or payroll setup as a side effect of the software switch, without talking to your CPA

A new file is not the same thing as a change in how you report income.

## Should you set up or switch now, or wait?

A new system is worth doing for January 1, 2027 if the current one is getting in the way: books that stay behind, reports you do not trust, a spreadsheet that has reached its limit, or a need for payroll, inventory, job costing, or a bookkeeper who requires a proper file. Many businesses use QuickBooks Online; others use QuickBooks Desktop, a spreadsheet, or another program, depending on their transactions, reporting needs, and who will keep the books.

It may be wiser to wait, or to clean up in place first, if you cannot yet trust this year's opening balances, you are in the middle of a sale or refinance, the real problem is delayed recordkeeping, or you are deciding too late in December to set the file up carefully.

Luke 14:28 says, "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?" (ESV). If you cannot finish the setup well, wait for a later clean break rather than rush a messy conversion.

If you do move forward:

1. Finish, or substantially complete, your 2026 year-end bookkeeping
2. Choose the system with your CPA or bookkeeper, and map the chart of accounts
3. Gather the opening balances and supporting detail described above
4. Build the new file and match the January 1 balance sheet to December 31, 2026
5. Put new activity only in the new file
6. Reconcile January in full, and keep the old file available

## Faithful stewardship of a new year's records

1 Corinthians 14:40 says, "But all things should be done decently and in order" (ESV). For a business owner, that includes keeping the books in order as you start a new year. A clean start to 2027 gives you a clearer view of the business and a better basis for planning. Faithful stewardship includes setting up the file correctly and reconciling the first month. Sound financial guidance starts from books you can rely on.
